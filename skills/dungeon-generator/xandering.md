# Xandering — Non-Linear Dungeon Structure

The craft this file teaches was named and championed by Justin Alexander
(["Xandering the Dungeon"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/13085/roleplaying-games/xandering-the-dungeon),
originally "Jaquaying," after Jennell Jaquays, whose *Caverns of Thracia* and
*Dark Tower* exemplify it). The techniques below are taught in this repo's
own words and examples; mainstream DM guidance lands on the same conclusions
— see `dungeon-design.md`'s layout principles.

**Why:** when the map is a single path, every fork is decoration — the group
walks the route the designer walked, in order, every time. A multiply
connected site hands those choices back: scouting the approach, backing out
of a bad fight and circling in another way, returning by a different door,
and a different run for every group that enters. It costs nothing at the
table — a well-connected map runs room by room exactly like a sparse one;
the map is a flowchart either way.

## The floor — every dungeon must pass

Check these off the finished **edge list** (not the prose — count the edges):

1. **≥ 2 entrances.** The approach is the first strategic choice, and a
   second entrance is a loop through the surface.
2. **≥ 1 loop.** Two branches hooked together. If the topology reduces to a
   line or a tree, it fails — trace it: from any room, is there more than one
   way back to the entrance?
3. **No secret-gated spine.** Critical-path progress — reaching the objective
   at all — never sits behind a single hidden or missable thing. Secrets gate
   bonuses, shortcuts, and treasure (the hide-extras rule in
   `dungeon-design.md`: it's fine to hide *extras* where they might never be
   found; never essentials).
4. **The objective sits deep, reachable by ≥ 2 routes.** As far from the
   entrances as the topology allows, with at least two distinct routes to it,
   each costing something different — fights, hazards, the dungeon mechanic,
   a favor.

## The signature technique — pick exactly one

Beyond the floor, each dungeon commits to **one** signature technique and
explores variations of it. That's how Jaquays actually worked (*Thracia*
commits to vertical, multi-level space and develops that theme throughout) —
one technique explored deeply beats all twelve crammed in, and it's what
makes each dungeon structurally memorable. Offer 2–3 candidates grounded in
the anchor's fiction; the DM picks.

## The twelve techniques

Grouped by what each buys the party. Modern-interior manifestations in
parentheses — translate to whatever the setting provides.

### Choosing the approach

1. **Many ways in** — several distinct entrances, some earned or hidden.
   Each is both an opening move and a way back out (street doors, loading
   docks, a roof hatch, a storm-drain grate).
2. **Mid-structure arrival** — the obvious way in lands partway up the
   stack, with territory above *and* below from the first room (walking into
   a parking garage at street level: up and down both live).

### Keeping routes plural

3. **Route loops** — branches that rejoin so circuits exist; the basic
   building block of non-linearity (a service corridor ringing the floor,
   paired stairwells at opposite corners).
4. **Redundant level links** — more than one way between any two floors, so
   no level funnels into a chokepoint (the stairs, the freight shaft, and
   the ventilation chase all connect two and three).
5. **Level-skipping links** — a connection that bypasses floors entirely,
   letting a party leapfrog territory (an express elevator, a garbage chute
   to the sub-basement, a tunnel that surfaces a block away).
6. **Split levels** — a floor that can't be fully crossed without leaving it
   through another floor (the middle of level two is flooded; the far half
   is reached from above).

### Rewarding the curious

7. **Hidden byways** — paths that pay off searching and lateral thinking,
   beyond the classic concealed door (a squeeze through a cave-in, a
   floor-hatch drop, a flooded crawl, a machine that must be understood
   before it moves anyone — one-way passages count).
8. **Pocket levels** — small side-areas hanging off the main stack;
   discoveries in their own right or elaborate shortcuts (a mezzanine, a
   maintenance crawlspace, a walled-off annex).
9. **A dungeon inside a dungeon** — two fully realized complexes that
   happen to touch at a few seams, each with its own logic (an office
   tower whose sub-basements breach a much older tunnel system).

### Making space misbehave

10. **Half-flights and grades** — small elevation changes *inside* one
    level: ramps, short stairs, raised galleries. Keeps space
    three-dimensional and keeps player mapping honest (split-level floors,
    a drainage grade you only notice when the water runs).
11. **Impossible geometry** — space that doesn't add up: movement between
    levels the players can't detect, interiors that exceed their exteriors
    (a floor plan that can't be reconciled with the building you walked
    into).
12. **Elsewhere doors** — a section that isn't in the site at all (a door
    that opens somewhere else entirely; a room the building couldn't
    contain).

## The route-topology check (Melan lens)

A diagramming convention named for its originator, the ENWorld poster Melan:
to verify the floor, strip the dungeon to its decision structure — entrances
at the top, branches where routes split, edges where they rejoin. Ignore room
contents entirely. What remains should show visible loops and multiple
descents — if it untangles into a corridor with occasional dead-end twigs,
the dungeon is linear no matter how many doors it has.

## Payoffs to design for

- **Retreat and re-approach** are real options — a party that withdraws can
  come back a different way. Give lines of retreat teeth.
- **Route intel is treasure** — inhabitants, prisoners, and documents that
  reveal a secret path reward social play and searching (and are natural clue
  carriers).
- **The site reacts** — because routes are many, the inhabitants' response
  (alarms, patrols, restocking on a return visit) matters more than any
  scripted sequence. Pair with the active-roster notes in Step 5.
