# Dungeon Design — working principles

Site-design principles for any location with interior spaces to explore —
castle, tower, mansion, subterranean complex, or their modern equivalents.
This file carries *principles only* — every live rules number (door and
portcullis stats, secret-door DCs, trap mechanics, monster stat blocks and
XP, item text) comes from the sourcing chain in
[`rules-sourcing.md`](rules-sourcing.md) at runtime, never from here.

## The map is a decision graph

Whatever form the map takes, play moves through it as a sequence of choices:
which way in, which door, which floor. Design the choice structure first and
hang rooms off it — and give the graph real branches: more than one way into
the site, more than one exit from the rooms that matter. A map with no
choices left to make has stopped being a dungeon. (`xandering.md` builds its
structural floor on exactly this idea and makes it checkable.)

## History writes the floor plan

Ask two questions and most of the layout answers itself: **what was this
place built to do**, and **what has happened to it since**?

- **Built to do.** Every site had a job: house something precious, keep
  something in, keep something out, move something through (water, ore,
  freight, the dead), shelter a community, or hide an activity from view.
  The job implies the rooms — and implies which doors lock, and from which
  side.
- **Since then.** Nothing survives unchanged. Builders die out or move on;
  scavengers and squatters claim the shell; fire, flood, and collapse cut
  passages and open new ones; someone seals a wing and the reason is
  forgotten. Layer at least one such change over the original job — the
  mismatch between *built-for* and *used-for* is where exploration gets
  interesting.
- **Give every secret an author.** A hidden door was hidden *by* someone,
  *from* someone. Decide who and why, and the secret places itself — and
  the answer tells you what belongs behind it.

## Layout principles

- **Break the grid.** Rooms drawn by a ruler read as a diagram; irregular
  outlines and off-axis wings read as a place with a past.
- **Build up and down, not just across.** Stairs, ramps, shafts, ledges,
  and half-levels make both exploration and combat spatial problems rather
  than corridor traffic.
- **Water and rock get a vote.** Where a stream, a fault, or a cave mouth
  meets built structure, the architecture has to answer — bridges, drains,
  braces, detours. Let the answer show.
- **Ruin rewires the map.** Collapse and decay don't just decorate — they
  close old routes and open new ones. Edit the topology, not the paint.
- **The way in is not the way to the prize.** Put what the party came for
  deep in the graph, so reaching it means traversing the site rather than
  raiding its lobby.

## Rooms remember their users

- **Span needs support.** Large interior spaces need pillars, arches, or
  braced ceilings — which are also cover, climbing routes, and things that
  can fall.
- **Every occupant needs a workable door.** A creature that can't turn a
  handle didn't move in behind three of them: strong ones smash their way
  through, burrowers dig their own, and the room's entrances should say so.
  Check each lair against its occupant.
- **People write on walls.** Sapient tenants decorate — statues, murals,
  painted tallies — and everyone who passed through since left warnings,
  route marks, and graffiti. Wall-writing is a natural clue vector.
- **Stock rooms by job.** Where they slept, ate, worked, prayed, stored,
  and buried — and for each, which way the locks face and who was meant to
  be kept out (or in). For posted guards, know how many, how alert they
  are, and what they *do* when they notice intruders — rush, negotiate,
  raise an alarm, run for help.

## The site is an ecosystem

An inhabited location runs on needs: food, water, air, and safety, with the
strongest claiming the best real estate and predators posted where prey must
pass. Give the site an **internal logic players can reason from** — a fresh
pool means drinkers, which means an ambush spot; closed doors restrict which
creatures can move where. When players deduce something true about the site
from its logic, the design is working.

## Hide extras, never essentials

Hide bonus treasures and delightful surprises freely — but **never hide
anything essential** where the party might simply not find it. Provide hints
that something is findable: subtle (a rattle in the drawer) or obvious
(footprints ending at a blank wall). Hints let players find secrets without
strip-searching every room — and they double as the "Tells" line of the
dungeon mechanic's rules box.

## Hazards mean something

A trap that could be anywhere belongs nowhere. Hazards that feel random
teach players to check every flagstone, and the game slows to a crawl; the
antidote is placement that says something about the site's purpose or its
inhabitants, plus time pressure that keeps bold play rational (the
mechanic's clock, the roster's response). If the party can read *why* a
hazard is where it is, caution becomes a choice instead of a ritual.
