# Complications — the menu

A **complication** is the thing that makes a fight a *situation* instead of a
hit-point race. Every generated encounter carries **at least one**, chosen to
fit the node, the enemies, and the campaign's setting. Prefer one that
reframes the *objective* over one that just adds damage — a complication the
players have to *think* about beats one they only have to survive. Among the
rest, prefer one *both sides can interact with* — an element whose control can
shift mid-fight — over a static modifier: a second interactive element doesn't
add to the fight's possible states, it multiplies them.

Pick by fit, not by rolling down the list: legible (players grasp it fast),
changing a real decision, and part of the fiction rather than a bolted-on
rule. Telegraph every complication openly as the fight opens — a
complication the players never see is a gotcha, not a complication.

## Objective beyond "kill them all"

The strongest lever — give the fight a win condition that isn't "drop every
enemy," so victory is measured against the situation rather than the
initiative order:

- **Leave with the prize.** The party is there to take something — an
  object, a person, a fact — and get out. The enemies win by delaying;
  the party wins by leaving, not by clearing the room.
- **The clock decides, not the bodies.** Something finishes in *N* rounds —
  a rite completes, a door seals, a signal goes out, a captive is moved.
  Stalling is all the enemies need; the party has to push through the line
  and *act*, because winning the attrition war is losing.
- **Someone must still be standing.** A person, object, or position has to
  survive the fight. The party can't play purely for their own safety, and
  the enemies get to threaten the thing instead of the PCs.
- **The leash and the puppets.** The bodies in the way aren't willing —
  charmed locals, thralls, the coerced. Cutting them down is a cost the
  fiction will collect on; the real win is finding the leash-holder and
  cutting the leash.
- **The head behind the wall.** One target matters, screened by forces the
  party can't afford to grind through. Punch a hole and reach them — or
  withdraw and come back with a better angle.
- **Get everyone out.** The fight is already lost or not worth having; the
  win is a clean withdrawal — holding a door, covering the slow, paying
  for every round of extraction. Retreat as an objective, not a rout.

## Terrain & the battlefield

Static features that make *where you stand* a decision every round:

- **High ground worth taking** — ledges, balconies, scaffolds, a canyon
  rim. Reward climbing, jumping, and flight, and force melee to commit to
  a route up.
- **Cover worth fighting over** — positions that are genuinely hard to
  shoot into or reach, held by whoever got there first; the counter is
  repositioning, not plinking from safety.
- **A reason to leave your square** — features that punish the static and
  pay the bold: swinging fixtures, a gas main, a parked tanker, cargo that
  rolls when cut loose.
- **Space that won't hold still** — a chokepoint that blunts the bigger
  side's numbers, or a battlefield changing shape mid-fight: a draining
  pool, a floor going soft, water rising in a tunnel.
- **Broken sightlines** — pillars, smoke, stacked freight; a field where
  stealth, flanking, and repositioning beat a straight firing line.

## Contested assets

Battlefield elements that clearly help whichever side holds them. Taking one
splits attention from the objective; losing one hands the edge across:

- **Emplaced weapon** — a ballista, turret, or vehicle-mounted gun anyone can
  crew. Give it a fixed arc, limited ammunition, or a power source, so it's
  part of the place rather than future party loot.
- **Visible cache** — potions, ammunition, scrolls in a marked spot both sides
  can reach. (Tear the labels off and it becomes a chaos element.)
- **Boon spot** — a zone or font granting a temporary edge — advantage for a
  round, temporary HP — to whoever stands in it or taps it.
- **The cell and the alarm** — captive allies one side can free, or a
  summoning device either side can trigger; what answers arrives as
  reinforcements (see Dynamic pressure).

## Dynamic pressure

The fight gets worse if the party dawdles:

- **Reinforcements / waves** — a second group arrives on a timer or when an
  alarm is raised; rewards speed and silencing the caller.
- **Environmental hazard** — a spreading fire, green slime, live wires, smoke
  that blocks sight; a danger either side can turn to advantage.
- **Resource drain before the main event** — the fight opens with the party
  already taxed (a chase, a prior skirmish), so the budgeted threat bites
  harder.

## Chaos elements

Tempting features whose outcome *neither side* controls. Their presence and
danger must be obvious — only the outcome is unknown, so the informed choice
the players make is whether to gamble:

- **Unlabeled controls** — levers, switches, an unmarked console; someone was
  working them until the party cut them off. Pulling one does *something*.
- **The caged wildcard** — a confined creature either side can release; once
  out, it attacks anyone.
- **The tempting device** — obviously powerful, obviously not understood: a
  glowing orb, a humming machine, a "do not touch" sign. Activating it
  triggers an effect the DM picks or rolls, and it can escalate each round it
  stays on.

## Setting complications — draw from the campaign

What makes a fight feel like *this* campaign, not a generic dungeon. Pull the
specifics from the node's page and the campaign's setting; the patterns below
are the shapes to look for:

- **Witnesses.** The fight is somewhere it can be seen — a crowd, bystanders, a
  public place. Going loud has consequences beyond the fight; the complication
  is *fighting discreetly* or managing what onlookers perceive.
- **Hidden layer.** If the setting has a layer most people can't perceive, the
  enemies or terrain may live in it — only some characters see the real shape of
  the fight while bystanders see something ordinary and wrong. Half the
  battlefield is invisible to half the world.
- **The law of the place.** Neutral ground, a binding bargain, a house with its
  own rules — breaking the local code mid-fight has consequences beyond HP.
  Violence may be *against the rules* of where it happens.
- **Worked terrain.** Doors, vehicles, machinery, multiple floors, cameras,
  crowds — the location itself as obstacle and weapon.
- **Mixed attitudes.** Not everyone present is an enemy. A third party can be
  talked into helping, talked out of fighting, or tipped either way mid-combat.
  Seed it from your rules source's reaction and personality tables.
- **Enemies who don't fight to the death.** Cowardly, greedy, or boastful foes
  flee, surrender, or parley when the fight goes against them — a live NPC carries clues a
  corpse doesn't.

## A note on clues

A fleeing or surrendering enemy, a controller revealed behind innocent
minions, a ritual object left behind — these are **clue-bearing outcomes**,
and a complication is a natural place to plant a lead toward another node
(the fight procedure's Step 6 surfaces this). Don't silently bury a clue
opportunity inside a kill-them-all fight; the usual clue-interpretability
bar applies.
